How to Get Rid of a Hot Tub in the Treasure Valley

Published August 20, 2026 · DJR Junk Removal · Meridian, Boise & the Treasure Valley

A dead hot tub is one of the few household items a strong friend and a borrowed pickup genuinely cannot solve. It is also one of the most common things we get called about.

Why it is harder than it looks

An empty acrylic spa looks like a shell you could tip on its side and walk away with. Three things get in the way.

  • It is heavier than it looks. A mid-size spa runs several hundred pounds dry — acrylic shell, plywood or composite cabinet, a foam-insulated body, plus the pump, heater and plumbing.
  • It rarely fits back out the way it came in. Most spas were craned or carried in before a fence, a deck, a pergola or a hot tub pad existed around them. The clean path in is often gone.
  • It is wired in. Most are hardwired to a dedicated 240V circuit with a disconnect. That is an electrical job, not a hauling job.

Before anyone touches it

  1. Have the power disconnected properly. Turn off the breaker, and have a licensed electrician disconnect and make the circuit safe. Do not skip this step and do not guess at it.
  2. Drain it completely. Water is by far the heaviest part. Most tubs drain from a spigot at the base, though a submersible pump is faster. Give it longer than you think — a partially drained tub is still carrying serious weight, and the residual will end up on your patio.
  3. Clear the path. Gates, planters, patio furniture, low branches. Measure the narrowest point on the route out. That measurement decides everything about how the removal goes.

Drain it where it will not cause a problem. Spa water carries chlorine or bromine and can hold a fair amount of it. Do not send it into a storm drain or onto a neighbor's yard. If you are not sure where it should go, ask your local utility — the answer varies by jurisdiction and it is a quick question to settle.

Your three real options

1. Sell it or give it away

Worth trying first if it still runs. A working spa has some value, and someone with a trailer may take it off your hands. Be honest in the listing about its condition — and be realistic that "buyer must remove" is exactly the reason most of these listings sit.

2. Break it down yourself

Doable, and genuinely unpleasant. Once the power is disconnected and it is drained, the cabinet comes off, the equipment bay comes out, and the shell gets cut into pieces you can carry. Expect a reciprocating saw, eye and ear protection, gloves, and a full day. Then you still have to get the pieces to a transfer station — and cut acrylic and insulation foam make a mess that travels.

3. Have it hauled

What we do. We break it down on site and carry it out in pieces, which is what makes it possible past a narrow gate or off a raised deck. You do not need to do anything except have it drained and the power safely disconnected before we arrive.

What it costs

A hot tub is priced by the space it takes up in the truck once it is broken down, on the same volume tiers as any other job — a spa on its own typically lands in the $275 to $389 range depending on size, with access and disposal factored in. Larger swim spas and units in genuinely difficult spots are quoted individually.

The most useful thing you can send us is a photo of the tub and a photo of the route out to the driveway. Those two pictures answer nearly every question we would otherwise have to ask.
